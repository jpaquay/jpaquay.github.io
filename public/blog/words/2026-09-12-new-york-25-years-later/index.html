New York, Twenty-Five Years Later: The Light We Carry Together

From 'Then it went dark. Real dark. Like snow.' to a quarter-century of shared human memory, resilience, and inclusive solidarity across oceans.

Twenty-five years ago today—on September 12, 2001, when the air over Lower Manhattan was still thick with gray dust and the world felt suspended in an impossible silence—I published a poem titled simply New York.

Written in the raw aftermath of September 11, those verses ended with seven words that captured the shock of a stunned planet:

September 12, 2001: "Then it went dark. Real dark. Like snow."
Read the original post written 25 years ago: New York (2001-09-12).

A quarter of a century has passed since that autumn morning. An entire generation has grown into adulthood knowing that day only through history books, quiet memorials, and the stories of those who were there. Yet whether we stood on the sidewalks of New York, watched in disbelief from Brussels, Paris, London, or Tokyo, or inherited the memory through family and community, we all remember.

Twenty-five years later, remembrance is no longer just an echo of shock. It has matured into an inclusive moment—a shared human covenant across oceans, cultures, and generations. On that morning, citizens from more than 90 nations—speaking dozens of languages, holding every faith, and carrying dreams from every corner of the earth—were lost side by side. Because the loss belonged to the entire human family, the healing and the memory belong to all of us too.

Answering the Questions of 2001

In the original poem written on September 12, 2001, a voice in the ash asked:

who will tell today
who will weep today
who will tell tomorrow tomorrow
will any poem some day ever carry sufficient weight
to leave the script of scraps recalling fall and forgetting

Today, on September 12, 2026, here is the answer we write together—not in darkness, but in the twin columns of light that pierce the September night:

—words twenty-five years later

we tell tomorrow together now
across twenty-five autumns of wind and tide
the snow of ash has long returned to earth
yet not a single syllable of love was lost

you asked who would carry sufficient weight
when glass and steel gave way to sky
no solitary poem could lift that stone—
so millions of quiet hands reached out to hold it

in the footprints where the towers stood
dark granite cradles rushing water
falling forever into depth, yet never empty
bronze holds the names warm beneath the fingers of strangers
a mother from Queens, a father from Accra, a clerk from Brussels
neighbors in the indivisible republic of grief

look beside the cascading water:
the pear tree pulled charred from the October rubble
its trunk still wears the black seam of the fire
yet every April it crowns itself in white blossoms
teaching the concrete that survival is not forgetting—
it is blooming with the scar intact

when twilight settles on the harbor now
two sapphire pillars climb the stratosphere
not to summon the dark back down
but to show how high human memory can reach
when we refuse to let fear close our doors

then it went dark. real dark. like snow.
and in that dark, we learned to be the light.

The Survivor Tree: Growing With the Scar Intact

If there is one living emblem of these past twenty-five years, it stands near the southwest corner of the South Pool at the National September 11 Memorial: the Survivor Tree.

In October 2001, recovery workers clearing the smoking debris of the World Trade Center plaza discovered a crushed Callery pear tree. Its roots were snapped, its bark was blackened by fire, and only a single living branch remained. Rather than discard it, workers carefully extricated the tree and sent it to the New York City Department of Parks and Recreation nursery in Van Cortlandt Park in the Bronx.

For nine years, horticulturists tended to the wounded tree. Slowly, its roots took hold. New branches grew upward from the jagged, burned stump. In December 2010, the tree—now thirty feet tall—was returned to the memorial plaza.

If you look closely at its trunk today, you can see the unmistakable transition: below is gnarled, scarred, battle-worn bark; above is smooth, vigorous wood that bursts into clouds of delicate white blossoms every spring. Furthermore, saplings propagated from the Survivor Tree’s seeds have been gifted to communities around the world that have endured tragedy—creating a living, global network of mutual empathy.

It is the most honest metaphor we possess for the human heart after trauma. Resilience does not mean pretending the fire never happened; it means growing tall enough to shelter others while carrying the memory of what we endured.

An Inclusive Moment: What 25 Years Have Taught Us

In the immediate days after September 11, 2001, an extraordinary wave of global solidarity swept the world. From candlelight vigils on the Grand-Place in Brussels to spontaneous memorials outside embassies from Dublin to Seoul, millions of strangers stood shoulder to shoulder. When Jean-Marie Colombani penned his famous editorial in Le Monde on September 12—“Nous sommes tous Américains”—it resonated because it spoke to an elemental truth: in moments of profound vulnerability, humanity has no borders.

Over the quarter-century that followed, the world was tested by many storms—periods where fear, polarization, and suspicion threatened to erode that initial grace. Yet whenever September returns, and the twin beams of the Tribute in Light rise into the Manhattan twilight, we are called back to our better angels:

  1. Memory is Inclusive, Never Exclusive: Grief has no nationality. Honoring the victims of 9/11—and the heroic firefighters, paramedics, police officers, ironworkers, and volunteers who ran toward danger—means honoring the dignity of every human life, everywhere.
  2. Empathy Across Generations: To those born after 2001, this anniversary is an invitation to inherit not the trauma of that day, but the solidarity of September 12—the day strangers shared water, opened their homes, donated blood in lines that stretched around city blocks, and looked out for one another.
  3. Peace as an Active Architecture: Just as we build resilient systems and foundations in our daily work, peace and human connection require deliberate, daily care. Every act of kindness, inclusion, and bridge-building across cultures is a quiet tribute to those we lost.
